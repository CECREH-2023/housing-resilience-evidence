# Technology, Decaying Infrastructure, and Housing Resilience Research 2006–2026

## Scope and definitions

This report synthesizes English-language research (2006–2026) on how **technology** and **decaying infrastructure** shape **housing resilience**, i.e., the ability of housing (individual dwelling units, multifamily buildings, and aggregated housing systems) to **maintain habitability**, **withstand hazards**, and **recover function** after disruptive events. The focus is on:

- **Technology pathways**: smart-home and smart-building technologies (sensors/IoT, building controls, ICT and communication), structural health monitoring (SHM), decision-support platforms, advanced materials and retrofit technologies, distributed energy resources (DERs) such as solar+storage for outage backup, and related data/analytics.
- **Decaying infrastructure pathways**: aging utilities (electricity, water/wastewater; and their interdependencies), structural degradation and deterioration of building components, deferred maintenance backlogs, and the compounding effects of underinvestment (including public/social housing stock).

Because “housing resilience” is studied across many disciplines (civil engineering, building science, housing policy, disaster recovery, public health, energy systems), the core corpus spans peer-reviewed journals, conference proceedings, and manifest-listed standards where they directly inform housing habitability, housing recovery, or housing-related lifeline performance. A short contextual section at the end separates additional institutional guidance and reports that are useful background but are not counted in the 22-item core corpus.

## Search strategy and inclusion criteria

This is a **structured, bounded open-web scan** conducted on **April 4, 2026** (America/Chicago). Full reproducible queries inside subscription bibliographic databases (e.g., **Web of Science** or **Scopus**) were not available for this pass, so the dataset should be treated as a **high-relevance curated corpus** rather than a guaranteed “all papers” census.

**Sources searched (open web equivalents and primary/official pages):**
- Publisher platforms and DOI landing pages where accessible (e.g., ScienceDirect/Elsevier, Springer, Taylor & Francis pages).
- Open-access repositories and official institutional publication portals (e.g., NIST publications, LBNL/DOE publication portals, federal agency PDFs).
- Open full-text mirrors (e.g., PubMed Central where relevant) for methods/sample extraction.

**Representative keyword families (examples):**
- Housing resilience AND (smart home OR sensors OR IoT OR ICT OR “smart building” OR monitoring OR “structural health monitoring” OR retrofit OR “advanced materials”)
- Housing OR residential buildings AND (power outage OR water outage OR “lifeline infrastructure” OR interdependen* OR cascading failure)
- Public housing OR social housing AND (deferred maintenance OR capital backlog OR aging infrastructure OR habitability OR indoor environment sensors)
- Post-disaster housing recovery AND (model* OR Monte Carlo OR agent-based OR decision support OR permitting OR inspections OR insurance)

**Inclusion criteria:**
- 2006–2026; English; peer-reviewed journal articles and conference papers, plus major reports/standards with direct relevance to housing resilience planning, retrofit, monitoring, habitability, and housing recovery.
- Explicit linkage to either (a) technology enabling resilience, (b) deterioration/aging/deferred maintenance undermining resilience, or (c) housing outcomes driven by lifeline disruptions (power/water/heat) and infrastructure interdependencies.

**Exclusion criteria (typical):**
- Purely conceptual “resilience” papers with no housing/building/habitability relevance.
- Non-English or inaccessible items without sufficient bibliographic verification.
- Non-peer-reviewed commentary/blog content unless it is a major institutional report (e.g., official agency guidance).

## Executive synthesis of trends, gaps, and top-cited works

Across 2006–2026, the literature converges on a core insight: **housing resilience is socio-technical**—it depends simultaneously on (1) the **physical dwelling/building**, (2) the **lifeline services** that make homes habitable (power, water, heat, communications), and (3) the **institutions and policies** that govern maintenance, retrofits, inspections, finance, and recovery timelines. A consistent theme is that **technology can mitigate** resilience failures (early detection, monitoring, backup power, decision-support), but **deferred maintenance and aging systems can dominate outcomes** by increasing baseline fragility and delaying recovery.

### Major trends

**Monitoring and sensing moved from feasibility to deployment barriers and governance.** Early SHM and wireless sensing work emphasized feasibility and architecture (e.g., wireless sensors and networks, prototype platforms), motivated by the reality of aging and degrading structures. Later contributions emphasized the persistent “deployment gap” (standards, reliability, value cases, data pipelines), including calls to close research-to-practice barriers.

**Housing resilience increasingly incorporates habitability under outages (“thermal resilience” and essential services).** Building-science work reframed resilience around **safe indoor conditions during extreme weather and possible coincident outages**, pushing metrics, modeling workflows, passive survivability, and policy levers (including backup power and passive design).

**Retrofitting shifted toward integrated packages (energy + hazard), not single-objective upgrades.** Multiple strands show that retrofits aimed purely at efficiency may not be robust under climate change, and that integrated seismic+energy retrofit is an emerging strategy (exoskeletons, envelope replacement, integrated interventions), though full-scale validation gaps remain.

**Decision-support platforms increasingly model buildings + infrastructure + households as interdependent systems.** Community-scale platforms (e.g., IN-CORE) and related methods link building inventories, lifeline damage, recovery functions, social vulnerability/household models, and economic modules to simulate cascading impacts and recovery pathways—bridging “housing” and “infrastructure” resilience in one computational chain.

**Evidence from public/social housing highlights how infrastructure failures translate into health and evacuation outcomes.** Empirical qualitative work on high-rise public housing after Hurricane Sandy documents prolonged outages (power/heat/water), constrained evacuation feasibility, and compounded vulnerability—illustrating that decaying systems and lifeline failures can deplete household “resilience reserves.”

**Deferred maintenance is consistently described as compounding risk and cost.** Major housing stock assessments and more recent cost syntheses emphasize that deferred maintenance and aging systems increase capital needs and can accelerate deterioration, affecting long-term viability and resilience of the public housing portfolio. Parallel infrastructure literature (e.g., water utilities) describes how aging assets and deferred maintenance threaten service reliability—directly affecting housing habitability when outages occur.

### Gaps and under-covered areas

**Rigorous evaluations of smart-home / sensor interventions in low-income housing remain scarce.** Despite promising feasibility findings for indoor environment sensors in social housing, the broader evidence base still lacks large-scale causal evaluations on outcomes like reduced damp/mold exposure, avoided emergencies, or reduced outage harms.

**Data governance, privacy, and consent are often treated as secondary, yet they are central to scaling.** Studies on scaling smart home performance monitoring explicitly argue that regulatory and data-access frameworks can constrain implementation, even when technology works.

**Interdependency modeling often outpaces operational integration.** While community resilience platforms can model interdependencies, operationalizing those insights into permitting reforms, contractor capacity planning, or utility restoration prioritization remains an implementation challenge, especially for equity-oriented housing outcomes.

**Citation-count comparisons are not standardized.** Citation counts vary by database (publisher “cited by,” PubMed, Google Scholar, etc.); this report therefore treats citation visibility as indicative rather than as a normalized bibliometric measure.

## Annotated bibliography with methodology, region, data, and implications

The project bibliography provides structured metadata; the entries below add a one-paragraph synthesis of **what each work contributes** to the intersection of (a) technology, (b) aging/decay/deferred maintenance, and (c) housing resilience.

**Wireless/IoT sensing and structural condition technologies**

Lynch, J.P., & Loh, K.J. (2006). *A Summary Review of Wireless Sensors and Sensor Networks for Structural Health Monitoring*. **The Shock and Vibration Digest**, 38(2), 91–128. DOI: 10.1177/0583102406061499. This early review frames wireless sensing as a cost-reducing alternative to tethered monitoring, motivated by aging and deterioration of structural systems; it highlights how dense sensing and embedded processing can support damage detection and condition-based maintenance, which are directly relevant for sustaining housing safety and accelerating post-event assessment where building integrity governs habitability and re-occupancy decisions.

Chintalapudi, K.K., Paek, J., et al. (2006). *Monitoring Civil Structures with a Wireless Sensor Network*. **IEEE Computer**, 39(3). DOI: 10.1109/MIC.2006.38. This demonstration-oriented work is representative of a class of research that operationalizes wireless sensing for civil structures, aiming to make monitoring practical at scale; the key housing-resilience linkage is that lowering monitoring costs and enabling faster detection of deterioration can mitigate risks from deferred maintenance and can shorten the time needed to render safe/unsafe decisions after disasters affecting residential buildings and supporting infrastructure.

Severino, R., Gomes, R., Alves, M., et al. (2010). *A Wireless Sensor Network Platform for Structural Health Monitoring: enabling accurate and synchronized measurements through COTS+custom-based design*. **IFAC Proceedings Volumes**. DOI: 10.3182/20100908-3-PT-3007.00084. This paper focuses on the practical engineering constraints that make wireless monitoring deployable—especially synchronization and measurement accuracy—which matter for residential buildings because reliable monitoring is a prerequisite for linking observables (e.g., vibration signatures) to actionable maintenance decisions, helping prevent small defects from becoming large resilience failures.

Cawley, P. (2018). *Structural health monitoring: Closing the gap between research and industrial deployment*. **Structural Health Monitoring**, 17(5), 1225–1244. DOI: 10.1177/1475921717750047. This widely cited perspective explains why SHM systems often fail to translate into routine practice—highlighting technical reliability, data interpretation, and business-case barriers—which is directly relevant to housing resilience because the largest resilience benefit from monitoring comes only when monitoring is integrated into asset management and maintenance workflows for aging housing stock rather than treated as pilot technology.

Sofi, A., Regita, J.J., Rane, B., & Lau, H.H. (2022). *Structural health monitoring using wireless smart sensor network – An overview*. **Mechanical Systems and Signal Processing**, 163, 108113. DOI: 10.1016/j.ymssp.2021.108113. This review synthesizes the shift to wireless smart sensors and the growing coupling of SHM with AI/ML methods; it explicitly ties monitoring to repair/rehabilitation needs and notes a gap between academic advances and real-world execution—an important housing-resilience point because old residential buildings and multifamily systems often fail through gradual deterioration, and institutionalizing practical monitoring could reduce the frequency of unsafe conditions caused by deferred maintenance.

**Smart homes, indoor environment sensing, and scaling challenges**

Ding, D., Cooper, R.A., Pasquina, P.F., & Fici-Pasquina, L. (2011). *Sensor technology for smart homes*. **Maturitas**, 69(2), 131–136. DOI: 10.1016/j.maturitas.2011.03.016. This foundational review outlines direct environment sensing and “infrastructure-mediated” sensing inside homes, emphasizing that smart homes depend on networks of low-power sensors and embedded processors; for housing resilience, the key contribution is the framing of sensors as a means to detect unsafe or deteriorating living conditions (temperature, humidity, air quality, activity anomalies) and the warning that evidence on usefulness and cost-benefit is necessary before wide deployment—especially relevant to scaling resilience tech beyond pilots.

Kim, M.J., Cho, M.E., & Jun, H.J. (2020). *Developing Design Solutions for Smart Homes Through User-Centered Scenarios*. **Frontiers in Psychology**, 11, Article 335. DOI: 10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00335. This user-centered work demonstrates that the effectiveness of smart-home technologies depends on whether control and service design match residents’ daily needs; for housing resilience, it provides a caution that resilience features (alerts, dashboards, automated controls) will not reduce risk if they are not adopted, trusted, and usable—especially in high-stress contexts like extreme weather or outages.

Adeyeye, K. (2024). *From product to service – strategies for upscaling smart home performance monitoring*. **Building Research & Information**, 52(1–2), 107–128. DOI: 10.1080/09613218.2023.2225111. Based on a large survey (reported as n=972) and factor analytic framing, this paper argues that scaling monitoring requires packaging technical capability as services (comfort, cost savings, information), while also contending with policy, regulatory, and market frameworks—linking housing resilience to the enabling environment for data access, incentives, and grid/communications infrastructure rather than to devices alone.

Walker, T., Buckingham, S., Menneer, T., Tu, G., Elliott, L.R., Mueller, M., et al. (2024). *Indoor environment sensor systems for healthier homes: a feasibility study in social housing*. **Housing and Society**, 51(3), 260–286. DOI: 10.1080/08882746.2024.2386480. This feasibility study directly targets social housing, examining whether indoor sensor systems can improve property management and resident health; it is highly relevant to resilience because it operationalizes sensing as a tool for proactive repairs (e.g., responding to low temperatures or unhealthy interior conditions) and thus potentially reduces vulnerability created by poor housing quality and maintenance deficits, while also raising core implementation questions around consent, governance, and who acts on alerts.

**Retrofitting technologies, materials, and resilience performance**

Ascione, F., Bianco, N., De Masi, R.F., Mauro, G.M., & Vanoli, G.P. (2017). *Resilience of robust cost-optimal energy retrofit of buildings to global warming: A multi-stage, multi-objective approach*. **Energy and Buildings**, 153, 150–167. DOI: 10.1016/j.enbuild.2017.08.004. This paper operationalizes “resilience” as robustness of retrofit performance under climate warming scenarios, using simulation and multi-objective optimization; it contributes to housing resilience by showing that retrofit choices can degrade in effectiveness as climate conditions shift, implying that programs focused on deferred maintenance and energy upgrades should explicitly account for future climate and habitability risks rather than optimizing for today’s conditions alone.

Burton, H.V., Deierlein, G., Lallemant, D., & Singh, Y. (2017). *Measuring the Impact of Enhanced Building Performance on the Seismic Resilience of a Residential Community*. **Earthquake Spectra**, 33(4), 1347–1367. DOI: 10.1193/040916EQS057M. By linking building inventory performance to community-level resilience metrics, this work shows how upgrading building performance (through design or retrofit) can yield quantifiable improvements at the community scale; for housing resilience, it helps bridge the gap between engineering performance targets and system-level recovery outcomes (i.e., how many homes remain safe/usable and how quickly).

Pertile, V., et al. (2021). *Seismic and Energy Integrated Retrofitting of Existing Buildings: A Critical Review*. **Sustainability**, 13(16), 9363. DOI: 10.3390/su13169363. This critical review consolidates integrated strategies that simultaneously address seismic vulnerability and energy performance, arguing that single-objective retrofits can miss synergistic opportunities; its resilience relevance is that integrated packages can reduce both hazard damage and outage-related habitability loss (through better envelopes), while policy relevance lies in coordinating normally separate funding and regulatory streams (energy efficiency vs hazard mitigation).

Pohoryles, D.A., Bournas, D.A., Da Porto, F., Caprino, A., Santarsiero, G., & Triantafillou, T. (2022). *Integrated seismic and energy retrofitting of existing buildings: A state-of-the-art review*. **Journal of Building Engineering**, 61, 105274. DOI: 10.1016/j.jobe.2022.105274. This state-of-the-art review maps integrated retrofit solutions—especially exoskeletons and envelope interventions—and highlights a research gap in large-scale experimental validation; for housing resilience, it provides an evidence base for prioritizing retrofit technologies that simultaneously reduce collapse risk and improve thermal/energy performance, which is especially critical for older housing stock where both structural degradation and energy inefficiency are common.

Hong, T., Malik, J., Krelling, A., O’Brien, W., Sun, K., Lamberts, R., & Wei, M. (2023). *Ten questions concerning thermal resilience of buildings and occupants for climate adaptation*. **Building and Environment**, 244, 110806. DOI: 10.1016/j.buildenv.2023.110806. This highly cited agenda-setting paper defines thermal resilience as maintaining safe indoor conditions during extreme weather that may coincide with outages; it emphasizes metrics, simulation workflows, passive and low-energy measures, backup power, and behavioral interventions—making it directly relevant to housing resilience in aging housing stock where poor envelopes and failing systems can turn outages into life-safety events, and it points to policy needs for thermal resilience requirements and incentives.

**Housing recovery modeling, decision-support, and socio-technical resilience**

Perrucci, D., & Baroud, H. (2020). *A Review of Temporary Housing Management Modeling: Trends in Design Strategies, Optimization Models, and Decision-Making Methods*. **Sustainability**, 12(24), 10388. DOI: 10.3390/su122410388. This review consolidates models for temporary housing design and allocation, emphasizing optimization and decision methods; the resilience connection is that housing resilience is not only preventing damage but also ensuring people can be housed safely during recovery, and technology-enabled planning tools can reduce displacement duration and coordination failures.

Wang, W.L., & van de Lindt, J.W. (2021). *Quantitative modeling of residential building disaster recovery and effects of pre- and post-event policies*. **International Journal of Disaster Risk Reduction**, 102259. DOI: 10.1016/j.ijdrr.2021.102259. Using multi-layer Monte Carlo simulation, this paper decomposes recovery into downtime due to delay (permits, inspections, insurance) and downtime due to repair; for housing resilience, it offers a quantitative way to test policy levers that can reduce recovery time—critical when aging infrastructure and limited inspection/contractor capacity compound delays for older homes.

Hofmann, S.Z. (2022). *Build Back Better and Long-Term Housing Recovery: Assessing Community Housing Resilience and the Role of Insurance Post Disaster*. **Sustainability**, 14(9), 5623. DOI: 10.3390/su14095623. This comparative case study (New Orleans post-Katrina; Christchurch post-earthquakes) highlights how insurance and governance systems influence the onset and speed of recovery (“time compression”); for housing resilience, it shows that institutional processes can dominate long-term rebuilding outcomes even in affluent contexts—implying that technical retrofitting and monitoring must be paired with finance/claims process reforms to reduce prolonged habitability loss.

Hernández, D., Chang, D., Hutchinson, C., Hill, E., Almonte, A., Burns, R., Shepard, P., Gonzalez, I., Reissig, N., & Evans, D. (2018). *Public Housing on the Periphery: Vulnerable Residents and Depleted Resilience Reserves post-Hurricane Sandy*. **Journal of Urban Health**, 95(5), 703–715. DOI: 10.1007/s11524-018-0280-4. Based on eight focus groups (74 residents) in Sandy-affected public housing communities in NYC, this work documents how prolonged outages of heat, power, and water (lifeline failures) intersect with vulnerability and building conditions to shape evacuation decisions and health impacts; it is central to understanding how decaying infrastructure and deferred maintenance can amplify disaster impacts in multifamily public housing and why resilience investments must address both physical systems and social support structures.

Kuang, B., Shi, Y., Hu, Y., Zeng, Z., & Chen, J. (2024). *Household energy resilience in extreme weather events: An investigation of energy service importance, HVAC usage behaviors, and willingness to pay*. **Applied Energy**, 123051. DOI: 10.1016/j.apenergy.2024.123051. This survey-based study (n=485) empirically identifies which household energy services are least compromisable during disasters (thermal comfort, cooking, refrigeration) and models willingness-to-pay for maintaining services; it strengthens housing resilience practice by quantifying demand-side priorities that can guide rooftop solar+storage programs, outage planning, and targeted support for energy-burdened households—especially relevant where aging grids produce more outages.

Sun, T., Feng, Y., Zanocco, C., Flora, J., Majumdar, A., & Rajagopal, R. (2025). *Solar and batteries are affordable options for US households*. **Nature Energy**, 10, 928–929. DOI: 10.1038/s41560-025-01822-9. This piece frames rooftop solar and battery storage as practical outage backup for many households and emphasizes equitable access for high-outage-risk and energy-burdened communities; for housing resilience, it strengthens the argument that household-scale energy technologies can substitute for failing or stressed infrastructure during disruptions, but adoption will be uneven without policy intervention. 
Related research article: *Solar and battery can reduce energy costs and provide affordable outage backup for US households* (2025). DOI: 10.1038/s41560-025-01821-w.

**Interdependent infrastructure modeling and community-scale tools**

van de Lindt, J.W., Kruse, J., Cox, D.T., Gardoni, P., Lee, J.S., Padgett, J., McAllister, T.P., Barbosa, A., Cutler, H., Van Zandt, S., Rosenheim, N., Navarro, C.M., Sutley, E., & Hamideh, S. (2023). *The interdependent networked community resilience modeling environment (IN-CORE)*. **Resilient Cities and Structures**, 2(2), 57–66. DOI: 10.1016/j.rcns.2023.07.004. This paper describes a modular modeling environment that chains hazard, building/infrastructure damage, recovery, household/business dynamics, and economic impacts; the housing resilience value is that it supports “what-if” analysis at household/building resolution—linking technology-enabled analytics to decisions about retrofits, restoration priorities, and how lifeline disruptions impact housing stability.

Nofal, O.M., et al. (2025). *Methodology for Interdependent Population–Building–Infrastructure Posthazard Functionality Assessment for Communities*. **ASCE Journal of Structural Engineering**. DOI: 10.1061/JSENDH.STENG-13222. This work advances probabilistic formulations for posthazard functionality that explicitly couples population, buildings, and infrastructure—implemented for household-level analysis in computational environments; for housing resilience, it supports more realistic modeling of when homes become functional again given lifeline status and population needs, enabling evaluation of equity-aware restoration and mitigation strategies.

**Decaying infrastructure, deferred maintenance, and housing vulnerability evidence**

Wallis-Lage, C. (2017). *Deferred Maintenance and Aging Infrastructure Threaten Resilience*. **Journal AWWA**, 109(5). DOI: 10.5942/jawwa.2017.109.0005. Although oriented to water utilities, this article directly supports housing resilience analysis because water reliability is a non-negotiable housing habitability service; it frames deferred maintenance as eroding resilience by increasing failures and service disruptions, which then cascade into housing risk during disasters and extreme weather.

McAllister, T.P. (2015). *Community Resilience Planning Guide for Buildings and Infrastructure Systems, Volume I* (NIST SP 1190v1). DOI: 10.6028/NIST.SP.1190v1. This foundational planning guide provides a six-step method for communities to set performance goals that maintain social and economic function after disruptions, explicitly linking building performance and infrastructure recovery targets; it is central to housing resilience practice because it frames housing and building systems as interdependent with lifelines and emphasizes integrated planning and stakeholder coordination.

## Contextual planning and guidance sources outside the core manifest

The following institutional reports and practice guidance are **not counted in the 22-item technology-infrastructure core corpus** because they are not currently represented as rows in `data/papers_master.csv`. They are retained only as contextual background for policy and practice implications. If they are used as primary evidence in a future manuscript version, they should first be added to the bibliography with theme, status, and source metadata.

Finkel, M., et al. (2010). *Capital Needs in the Public Housing Program* (Revised Final Report). **HUD / Abt Associates**. Using a nationally representative physical inspection approach (548 properties across 140 housing authorities), this report defines and estimates backlog and accrual capital needs for public housing systems; its relevance is that housing resilience depends on maintaining mechanical/electrical/architectural systems in serviceable condition, and it provides background for linking degraded building systems and deferred maintenance to long-run fragility and potential disaster vulnerability.

Public and Affordable Housing Research Corporation (PAHRC). (2025). *Estimating the Cost to Preserve the Nation’s Public Housing Stock*. This synthesis emphasizes that the compounding effects of deferred maintenance, inflation, and aging building systems drive very large preservation costs; for housing resilience, it strengthens the argument that resilience is inseparable from long-term capital planning and that delayed repairs increase future costs and risk exposure for residents.

Public Buildings Reform Board (PBRB). (2026). *The Cost of Inaction: Deferred Maintenance in GSA’s Portfolio* (Interim Report). While focused on federal buildings rather than housing, this report is relevant as a rigorously framed demonstration of how deferred maintenance backlogs increase risk and cost; the conceptual lesson translates to housing asset management: underfunding maintenance accelerates deterioration, undermines performance, and increases vulnerability to disruption.

Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA). (Homeowner guidance). *Homeowner’s Guide to Retrofitting*. This guide translates hazard mitigation technologies into homeowner-scale retrofit options (e.g., structural improvements, flood mitigation measures); for housing resilience, it operationalizes the “technology” pathway as practical retrofits that reduce damage and speed re-occupancy after hazards, particularly important for older homes where deferred maintenance and outdated construction elevate risk.

National Association of State Energy Officials (NASEO). (2021). *Electricity-Water Critical Infrastructure Interdependencies*. This report details types of interdependencies between electricity and water/wastewater and provides steps for planning and mitigation; housing resilience depends on these interdependencies because homes require both water and power for core habitability functions, and failures can cascade between sectors during disasters and long outages.

Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL). (2023). *Enhancing Resilience in Buildings Through Energy Efficiency* (PNNL-32727 Rev.1). This report compiles evidence that efficiency measures and stronger energy codes have resilience co-benefits—particularly maintaining safer indoor temperatures for longer during outages; for housing resilience, it supports policies that treat efficiency upgrades as both decarbonization and resilience interventions, especially for vulnerable households.

Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA). (2024/2025). *Pre-Disaster Housing Planning Guide*. This guidance links pre-disaster planning to housing recovery operations, including the coordination of programs that may fund retrofits and resilient rehabilitation; its housing resilience contribution is in institutional preparedness—ensuring that when infrastructure disruption and housing damage occur, communities can coordinate temporary and long-term housing solutions more effectively.

## Limitations and completeness statement

This report provides a **curated, high-relevance 22-item core corpus** and a structured synthesis of key works at the intersection of smart technologies, retrofits, and the realities of aging infrastructure/deferred maintenance that shape housing resilience. Contextual institutional guidance is separated from the core corpus unless it is represented in the project bibliography. However:

- A truly **comprehensive** inventory of *all* relevant academic articles (2006–2026) would require database-native review workflows (WoS/Scopus), including export, deduplication, screening logs, and standardized citation-count extraction.
- Citation counts are **not normalized** across a single bibliometric database; they should be interpreted as indicative rather than definitive.
- Some entries (especially conference papers and certain publisher pages) have partial bibliographic fields available through open sources; where page ranges or issue identifiers were not reliably accessible, the DOI is prioritized as the stable identifier.

Despite these limits, the corpus is designed to be **actionable**: it covers (1) sensing and monitoring technologies, (2) retrofit technologies and integrated retrofit research, (3) decision-support and recovery modeling tying buildings to lifelines and household outcomes, and (4) major evidence on deferred maintenance and aging systems—together capturing the dominant mechanisms linking technology and decaying infrastructure to housing resilience.
